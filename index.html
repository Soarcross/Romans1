Context of the Book

Who wrote it, to whom, and why

Before we read, let's get our bearings. Knowing the setting helps the words make sense.

Discuss

What do you already know or think about Romans?

  • Collect answers without correcting. Favorite verses, "the Romans Road," "it's heavy theology," "never read it."
  • Affirm: many people find Romans intimidating, but it's a letter written to real people.

Author & Setting

WhoPaulapostle to the Gentiles
Whenc. AD 57about 25 years after the resurrection
FromCorinthmost likely
  • Paul: a former persecutor of the church, converted on the Damascus road, now an apostle to the Gentiles.
  • Around AD 57, near the end of his third missionary journey.
  • Most likely written from Corinth, before he takes the collection to Jerusalem (15:25–26). Phoebe from nearby Cenchreae probably carried the letter (16:1–2).

A church Paul had never visited

Jewish believers + Gentile believers
AD 49
Claudius expels the Jews from Rome
Later
Jewish believers return
AD 57
Paul writes to the church
  • Paul didn't plant this church and had never been there (1:13).
  • It was a mixed church of Jewish and Gentile believers.
  • AD 49: Emperor Claudius expelled Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2; Priscilla and Aquila were among them).
  • For a few years the church was mostly Gentile. When Jewish believers returned, imagine the tension: different foods, calendars, and assumptions. That tension sits behind much of the letter.

Why Paul Writes

1To explain the gospel
2To unite Jew and Gentile
3To gain support for Spain 15:24
  • Explain the gospel: Romans is the fullest explanation of the gospel Paul ever wrote.
  • Unite Jew and Gentile: one gospel means one people, with no first-class and second-class Christians.
  • Spain: Paul wants Rome to be his base and sending church for a mission to the western edge of the known world (15:24). He's introducing himself and his message.

  • 1–3 The Problem: everyone, Gentile and Jew, is under sin.
  • 3–5 The Solution: righteousness from God through faith in Jesus.

If you were writing to a church you’d never met, what would you want them to know first?

  • Let a few people answer.
  • Then: "Let's see what Paul chose to say first." He starts with who he is, and right away he's talking about Jesus.

Section 3 · 10 min

Called

Romans 1:1–7

Ask someone to read verses 1–7 aloud as the next two slides come up.

1Paul, a servant of Christ Jesus, called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God, 2which he promised beforehand through his prophets in the holy Scriptures, 3concerning his Son, who was descended from David according to the flesh 4and was declared to be the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by his resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord,

Romans 1:1–4

  • In Greek, verses 1–7 are one long sentence. Paul can't introduce himself without talking about Jesus.
  • Notice how quickly the focus moves from Paul to "his Son."

Paul’s Identity v1

He is aServant
He wasCalled
He isSet apart
  • Servant (doulos): literally "slave." He belongs to someone else. In the Old Testament, "servant of the Lord" was also an honored title (Moses, David).
  • Called: he didn't apply for the job. God chose him (Damascus road).
  • Set apart: possibly a wordplay. "Pharisee" means "separated one." He was once set apart for the law, and now he's set apart for the gospel.
  • Paul's identity comes from what God did, not from his résumé.

The Gospel Is…

1Promised in Scripture v2
2About the Son, descended from David v3
3Declared Son of God in power v4
4…by the resurrection v4
  • Promised beforehand: the gospel isn't new. It's the fulfillment of the Old Testament story.
  • About his Son: the gospel isn't mainly advice or a program. It's a person.
  • Descended from David: truly human, the promised king (2 Samuel 7).
  • Declared Son of God in power: the resurrection didn't make him the Son. It publicly displayed it, with power.
Discuss

Paul gives a concise summary of the gospel in verses 1–4. Can you put it in one sentence of your own?

  • Give 60 seconds of quiet to think, or have people pair up.
  • Listen for: promised in Scripture, Jesus as truly human (David) and truly divine (Son of God), the resurrection.
  • Possible example: "God kept his promise by sending his Son, the true King, who rose from the dead and is Lord."
5through whom we have received grace and apostleship to bring about the obedience of faith for the sake of his name among all the nations, 6including you who are called to belong to Jesus Christ, 7To all those in Rome who are loved by God and called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Romans 1:5–7

  • "The obedience of faith": faith that shows up in a changed life. Paul uses the phrase as bookends of the letter (1:5 and 16:26).
  • "Grace and peace": Paul blends a Greek greeting (charis) with a Hebrew one (shalom). That's a small picture of a church made of Jews and Gentiles.

Who You Are vv6–7

Called to belongto Jesus Christ
Lovedby God
Called to be saintsholy, set apart
  • The same word, "called," is used for Paul (v1) and for ordinary believers (v6–7).
  • Saint doesn't mean "super-Christian." It means "holy one," someone set apart by God. Every believer is a saint.
  • Note the order: loved and called come first, and the behavior follows.

Section 4 · 5 min

Paul’s Heart

Romans 1:8–15

Short section. Read it and notice Paul's attitude toward people he's never met.

8First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for all of you, because your faith is proclaimed in all the world. 9For God is my witness, whom I serve with my spirit in the gospel of his Son, that without ceasing I mention you 10always in my prayers, asking that somehow by God’s will I may now at last succeed in coming to you. 11For I long to see you, that I may impart to you some spiritual gift to strengthen you— 12that is, that we may be mutually encouraged by each other’s faith, both yours and mine.

Romans 1:8–12

  • Paul, the great apostle, says he expects to be encouraged by them (v12). Encouragement goes both ways.

Paul’s Heart for Them

v8Thankful
v9Praying
v12Mutualencouragement
  • Thankful: he starts with what God is doing in them, not with problems.
  • Praying: "without ceasing" for people he's never met.
  • Mutual: he's not above them. He needs them too.
13I do not want you to be unaware, brothers, that I have often intended to come to you (but thus far have been prevented), in order that I may reap some harvest among you as well as among the rest of the Gentiles. 14I am under obligation both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to the wise and to the foolish. 15So I am eager to preach the gospel to you also who are in Rome.

Romans 1:13–15

  • "Greeks and barbarians": cultured people and "outsiders," educated and uneducated. In other words, everyone.
  • "Under obligation" = in debt. Paul feels he owes the gospel to all kinds of people.
Under obligation · v14

I am under obligation both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to the wise and to the foolish.

  • Obligated: the gospel was given to him for others. He can't keep it to himself.
  • "To all": no group is too cultured or too "outside" to need it.
Discuss

Why would Paul feel he “owes” the gospel to people? Who are you “under obligation” to?

  • Analogy: if someone gave you a cure to deliver, you'd owe it to the sick, even though they didn't give it to you.
  • Push for specific names and places: family, coworkers, classmates, neighbors.

Section 5 · 10 min · The center

The Thesis

Romans 1:16–17

  • This is the heart of the whole letter. Everything in Romans unpacks these two verses.
  • Slow down here.
16For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. 17For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

Romans 1:16–17

  • Read it slowly, twice. Maybe have everyone read it together the second time.
  • Ask: "What words jump out?" Then walk through it phrase by phrase.
Phrase 1 of 5

“not ashamed”

Romans 1:16

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

  • Why would he need to say this? Rome was the capital of power, and the gospel's hero was a crucified Jew from a backwater province.
  • Crucifixion was the most shameful death in the Roman world. Paul admits it looks like "folly" (1 Corinthians 1:18–23).
  • Connect back to the opening icebreaker about embarrassment.
Phrase 2 of 5

“the power of God”

Romans 1:16

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

  • The Greek word is dynamis (where we get "dynamite").
  • The gospel doesn't just describe salvation. It does it. God's power is at work when the message is spoken.
  • Rome trusted military and political power. Paul says real saving power is in this message.
Phrase 3 of 5

“to everyone who believes”

Romans 1:16

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

  • Everyone: no ethnic, moral, or social qualification. The only condition is faith.
  • "Jew first": the order of history (God's promises came through Israel), not a ranking. Both Jew and Greek come in the same way.
  • This speaks directly into the Roman church's Jew–Gentile tension.
Phrase 4 of 5

“the righteousness of God is revealed”

Romans 1:17

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

  • Two sides: God is righteous (he does what is right and keeps his promises), and he gives a right standing to people who don't have one (3:21–26).
  • "Revealed": uncovered, made visible. Verse 18 will use the same word for wrath.
  • "From faith for faith": by faith from start to finish.
Phrase 5 of 5

“The righteous shall live by faith.”

Habakkuk 2:4

For I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith for faith, as it is written, “The righteous shall live by faith.”

  • Quoted from Habakkuk 2:4, a prophet asking how to trust God while disaster approaches.
  • The point: this isn't new. Faith has always been the way.
  • This verse changed Martin Luther's life and helped spark the Reformation.
  • "Live" covers both receiving life and the way we live: by trusting God every day.
Discuss

Why might someone in Rome be tempted to be ashamed of a message about a crucified Jewish man?

  • Listen for: crucifixion was for slaves and rebels. Jews were a mocked minority. Rome valued power, honor, and status.
  • Follow-up: "What makes the gospel embarrassing in our culture?"
Discuss

What’s the difference between the gospel being “true” and the gospel being “power”?

  • Something true can sit on a shelf, like a fact you agree with. Power changes things.
  • Ask: "Where have you seen the gospel actually change someone, not just inform them?"

Section 6 · 12 min

Why We Need It

Romans 1:18–32

  • Now the hard part. Paul turns from the good news to the diagnosis.
  • Tone: we read this about ourselves, not to point at "those people." Chapter 2 will turn the finger back on the reader.
18For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth. 19For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. 20For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse.

Romans 1:18–20

Read aloud. Point out "revealed" again, the same word as in v17. Righteousness and wrath are both being revealed.

The diagnosis · v18

Wrath against those who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth

  • God's wrath isn't a temper tantrum. It's his settled, just opposition to evil, the response of a loving God to what destroys his world.
  • "Suppress" = to hold down, like holding a beach ball underwater. It takes effort. People aren't ignorant of God. They push the truth away.
Clearly seen · v20

His eternal power and divine nature have been clearly perceived… So they are without excuse.

  • Creation is a billboard for God: you can see his power and divine nature (Psalm 19:1).
  • Creation doesn't tell you the whole gospel, but it tells you enough to know you should honor and thank him.
  • So no one can say, "I had no idea."
21For although they knew God, they did not honor him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking, and their foolish hearts were darkened. 22Claiming to be wise, they became fools, 23and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man and birds and animals and creeping things.

Romans 1:21–23

Notice the downward slide: knew God, then didn't honor or thank him, then futile thinking, then darkened hearts, then exchanged.

The root · v21

They did not honor him as God or give thanks to him.

  • The root sin isn't a specific behavior. It's ingratitude and refusing to give God his place.
  • Every sin downstream grows from this root: I take the gifts and ignore the Giver.
  • Ask: what would it look like to be a more thankful person this week?
Discuss

Why does Paul start with ingratitude instead of a specific behavior?

  • Behaviors are fruit, and ingratitude is the root. Sin is first about a broken relationship with God, then broken rules.
  • Ask: "How does ingratitude actually feel day to day? What does it lead to?"
24Therefore God gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to impurity, to the dishonoring of their bodies among themselves, 25because they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever! Amen.

Romans 1:24–25

Even in the middle of this, Paul breaks into worship: "who is blessed forever! Amen."

26For this reason God gave them up to dishonorable passions. For their women exchanged natural relations for those that are contrary to nature; 27and the men likewise gave up natural relations with women and were consumed with passion for one another, men committing shameless acts with men and receiving in themselves the due penalty for their error. 28And since they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them up to a debased mind to do what ought not to be done.

Romans 1:26–28

  • Read with care. Some in the room may carry personal stories here, so be gentle and clear.
  • Paul's point is the pattern: when we reject the Creator, even our desires get bent. That's true of all of us in different ways.
  • Keep moving. The next slides pull out the big patterns.

“Exchanged”

3×
v23the glory of the immortal God→images
v25the truth about God→a lie
v26natural relations→what is contrary to nature
  • Sin is an exchange, and always a bad trade: we give up something glorious for something lesser.
  • We were made to worship. We never stop worshiping. We just swap the object (v25: "the creature rather than the Creator").
  • Ask: "What modern 'images' do people trade God for?" (career, comfort, approval, romance, politics).

“God gave them up”

3×
v24…to impurity, in the lusts of their hearts
v26…to dishonorable passions
v28…to a debased mind
  • Notice: the wrath described here (v18) isn't fire from heaven. It's God letting go and handing people over to what they chose.
  • Each "exchange" is matched by a "gave them up." We trade God away, and God gives us what we traded for.
  • C.S. Lewis: in the end, either we say to God "Thy will be done," or God says to us, "thy will be done."
Discuss

What does it mean that God’s judgment can look like giving us what we want?

  • "Gave them up" three times. The punishment is getting what we chose, along with all its consequences.
  • Ask: "Have you ever gotten something you wanted and found it empty or destructive?"
  • This is serious, but it's also a warning given in love.

Romans 1:29–31

  • Read the list slowly. Let it land.
  • Point out what's sitting right next to "murder": envy, gossip, boasting, disobedience to parents. Paul doesn't separate "big" and "small" sins.
  • Nobody in the room gets to read this list as being about someone else.
32Though they know God’s righteous decree that those who practice such things deserve to die, they not only do them but give approval to those who practice them.

Romans 1:32

  • The final step: not only doing wrong, but celebrating it, calling evil good.
  • And yet "they know." The conscience still testifies even as it is suppressed.
Discuss

Which sins in this list are “respectable” ones we tend to excuse?

  • Gossip ("just sharing a prayer request"), envy, boasting, disobedience to parents, strife.
  • Why do we rank sins? It usually lets us feel better than someone else.

Tie It Together

Romans 1:16–32

Quick zoom-out before application.

Discuss

Why does Paul put the good news (16–17) right before the bad news (18–32)?

  • So we never hear the diagnosis without the cure in hand. The bad news is only bearable because the good news comes first.
  • The bad news also shows why the good news is necessary. A cure means nothing until you know you're sick.

We cannot save ourselves.

The gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes.

Romans 1:16

  • Let this sit for a moment of silence.
  • Chapter 1 shows the problem is bigger than we thought, and verse 16 shows God's power is bigger than the problem.
